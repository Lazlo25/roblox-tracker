# Combined conclusions

## The short answer

Games that fade by month three run on a **fixed supply**: a set number of maps, stages, scares and upgrades that players use up. The five survivors run on a **renewable supply**. Their rules produce new situations every round without the developer building them, and what players earn keeps its value instead of being made obsolete.

So each year makes a survivor stronger: more items in people's collections, more veterans, more discovered techniques, more friend groups that default to it, more videos. A clone copies the rules in a week but starts all of that at zero. It isn't competing with a game; it's competing with years of accumulated player investment, and it loses.

Simplicity isn't something these games overcame. It's what makes all of this possible. Simple rules leave room for emergent depth, can be understood from a ten-second clip, work on a phone, and are cheap to balance and extend.

## Why the decline hits at month three

- By weeks 6 to 8, the launch players have seen every map and bought or ground through every upgrade.
- Sessions get shorter and return visits less frequent.
- Roblox's discovery leans heavily on engagement signals, so the game stops being recommended just as creators stop making videos about it.
- Fewer new players arrive and servers thin out. In a multiplayer game, thinner servers mean less content, so the remaining players leave faster.

The survivors had something to replace each of those before it ran out:
- **New situations** from the loop itself.
- **New things to want** from the calendar.
- **New players** from creators.
- **Protection** for the population that makes the content.

## The six survival factors, ranked

### 1. The core loop makes its own content

This is the necessary condition. Nothing else on this list saves a game that lacks it. In all five, round 1,000 is different from round 1 without any new development:
- **Opponents:** Blade Ball's parry mind-games, FTF's chases, MM2's hidden roles reshuffled every round, FTAP's physics fights.
- **Recombination:** ToH's handmade sections in a new order every round.
- **Mastery:** a skill ceiling means the game changes as you improve. Player-discovered techniques like pallet-surfing, rocket throwing and the lift side-jump give veterans something to chase.

Additions in these games multiply rather than add. A new Blade Ball ability, FTAP toy or ToH section interacts with everything already there. A game without this is on a content treadmill, needing to build faster than players consume, and a small team loses that race every time.

### 2. The population is protected

When players are the content, losing them destroys the content for everyone else. Every survivor has a rule that keeps weaker and casual players from being driven out:
- **Blade Ball** limits paid power and disables abilities in the decisive 1v1.
- **FTAP** gives casual players safe zones.
- **FTF and MM2** keep spending mostly cosmetic.
- **ToH** lets everyone finish.

They also give everyone something to do: rescue a teammate, grab the dropped gun, collect candy, re-climb, watch the final 1v1.

The result is that these games become a friend group's default answer to "what should we play?" Friends of mixed skill can play together, roles rotate so the weaker friend still gets moments, and rounds are short enough to join mid-session. Being a friend group's default is worth years.

Clones fail this in one of two ways. They sell power, so casual players leave, and then the big spenders leave because there's no one left to beat. Or they leave sidelined players with nothing to do.

### 3. What players own keeps its value

This is the moat against clones. The survivors sell **sidegrades and cosmetics**, not stronger numbers. A new Godly doesn't make an old one worse; it builds on it by borrowing its shape and sound. Limited, retired, chroma and evolving items gain value, and trading lets players feel and use that value.

Power-creep games do the opposite. Every update weakens what players already own, so each update has to outdo the last. Old purchases become worthless, which teaches spenders to stop spending.

Skill, levels, clans and friendships compound the same way. When a clone launches, an MM2 player would be walking away from everything they've built. It's no coincidence that the two oldest games here, MM2 and FTF, have the strongest trading and collecting economies.

### 4. Purchases are seen at the moment that matters, and there's always more to buy

This is why spend per person is so high.
- **Visible at peak emotion:** a Blade Ball ability, an FTF hammer, an MM2 knife and kill effect are all seen by the person who just lost to them, the most motivated buyer in the server. In a lobby where the same players stay round after round, that's also status.
- **Several ways to spend:** a predictable coin ladder with upgrade tiers, a chance layer with an exclusive prize, an event pass with free and paid routes, bundles, and crafting sinks. Each type of spender has a track, and big spenders never run out.
- **Gameplay purchases are bounded:** where effects are sold, they're sidegrades with counters, switched off wherever they'd decide the outcome alone.

Clones sell things nobody sees, or sell power and lose their population, or have one shop that's bought out in a week.

### 5. A predictable calendar that adds a layer to the same loop

MM2's event template:
- a themed lobby
- a new collectible spawned in ordinary rounds
- a pass
- a box or bundle
- often a new map or mode

Its log shows Halloween and Christmas nearly every year, with more events added once the template was proven. FTF's 2026 log already shows five updates by October. Blade Ball packed a live event, a free trial, a co-op boss, clans and limited modes into months 2 to 4.

These events add a reason to replay the loop players already like rather than replacing it. That makes them cheap to build, and they don't split the player base. Between events, small balance and map changes reopen the meta for veterans.

Predictability is the hidden strength. Lapsed players need a known date to come back to, friend groups plan around Halloween, and creators schedule videos around release times. A game that goes quiet tells players it's dying, and they believe it.

### 6. Every round makes clips, and every update feeds creators

All five produce watchable moments as a side effect of normal play: the 1v1 climax, flinging someone into the sun, the last-second rescue, the dropped-gun dive, falling at 99%. Simple rules mean a viewer understands the game in seconds, so videos turn into new players.

Updates feed this deliberately: announced release times (creators stayed up until 3 and 4 AM), new items that look good on camera, live events with showtimes. FTF goes further with crossovers and Roblox platform events, which reach audiences YouTube doesn't.

This replaces the players who drift away. Clones get one wave of videos at launch and no reason for a second.

## How to build this into your games

### Seven tests before you build

If an idea fails any of these, fix it or drop it before writing code.

1. **Round 1,000:** Will round 1,000 feel different from round 1 because of other players, roles, randomization or physics, not because you built more?
2. **One sentence:** Can the rules be explained in one sentence and understood from a ten-second clip?
3. **Weakest player:** What rule keeps the worst player in the server, or a free player facing a big spender, wanting to stay this round?
4. **Additions:** Will each new item, ability or map combine with what exists, or just be one more thing to use up?
5. **Two years:** Will what you sell still matter in two years, or will your own next update make it obsolete?
6. **Clip:** Does an ordinary round produce at least one moment someone would film?
7. **Original:** Are you the first or clearly best version of this mechanic? If you're copying a hot game, change the core action or pick another idea, because you'll be fighting an incumbent's accumulated assets.

### Core loop rules

- Rounds last a few minutes and escalate to a climax (an accelerating ball, a 2x timer, a last survivor), then start fresh.
- No one sits idle for more than about 20 seconds without something to do or watch.
- Roles rotate wherever possible.
- Failure costs a round, never progress, and no player can get permanently stuck.
- Timing and physics leave room for techniques. Correct exploits gently, and keep discovered techniques unless they break fairness.
- Content is built as modular pieces that combine: abilities, toys, sections, map props.

### Monetization stack

**At launch:**
- Cosmetics that show at the moment of impact: kill and death effects, weapon skins, trails, sounds, emotes.
- One earnable currency with a price ladder of roughly doubling tiers (Blade Ball's 2k, 4k, 8k, 12k), each item with its own upgrade tiers, and Robux packs to speed things up.
- A pass that speeds up earning rather than winning, like MM2's Elite coin bag.
- Private servers.

**In the first two months:**
- A chance layer (wheel or crate) with one exclusive item and useful consolation prizes such as timed boosts. Show the odds before purchase; Roblox requires this for paid random items.
- An event pass with a generous free track and a paid buyout.

**Later:** crafting or merge recipes, color variants, and items that evolve with use.

**Rules:**
- Sell sidegrades, never upgrades. Any paid gameplay effect needs a counter and must be disabled wherever it would decide the outcome alone.
- Offer free trials of premium abilities, like Blade Ball's Infinity weekend.
- Give new items a lineage. Reuse beloved shapes and sounds so new items borrow prestige from old ones.

### The first 90 days

| When | What to ship |
|---|---|
| Launch | A polished core loop, a cosmetic shop, an earnable currency, a daily reason to return (quest or reward), private servers |
| Weeks 2–4 | A first balance patch based on how players actually play; the chance layer; a first limited item, announced as leaving |
| Weeks 4–8 | A first event: a new collectible in normal rounds plus a free/paid pass; one limited-time mode built from the core loop; a free trial weekend of a premium item |
| Weeks 8–12 | A system that compounds: clans with shared goals, or trading; a first live event with a set showtime; the first limited items retired |

Hold something back for weeks 8 to 12. A game that launches with everything has nothing left for month three, exactly when it needs something.

Build the item system to be trade-ready from day one, even if trading opens later: every item gets a unique ID, a rarity, and a limited/retired flag. When trading opens:
- **Scam protection:** budget for clear trade confirmations and a value guide like FTF's Collector's Guide.
- **Real-money trading:** expect it to appear off-platform. It breaks Roblox's Terms, so discourage it rather than build around it.

### After the first 90 days

- Run two anchor events every year (Halloween and Christmas) plus two to four smaller ones, all from the same template. Each one includes a limited set that retires.
- Bring back old maps and modes; it's nearly free content.
- Between events, ship balance and map tweaks, run polls on balance, and fix quality-of-life issues.
- Keep up mobile controls, performance and anti-exploit work. Most of your players are on phones.
- Announce release times in advance and give creators something filmable.
- Join Roblox platform events, and look for crossovers with games whose audience differs from yours.
- Serve your subcultures with private-server modes (duels, map select) and leaderboards.

### What kills games at month three

- Finite authored content as the main draw: fixed stages, story chapters, scripted scares.
- Power creep that makes old purchases worthless.
- Selling wins.
- Sidelined players with nothing to do.
- Points where a player can get permanently stuck.
- Events that replace the core loop instead of adding to it.
- Launching with everything and going quiet afterward.

## In one line

Build a loop where other players or recombination make the content, protect the players who make it, sell things that stay valuable and get seen, and give everyone a known date to come back.

# Final conclusions

There's no revenue data behind any of this, so these are bets. As you asked, I'm stating them plainly instead of hedging.

## The core answer

**These games are shops wrapped in a thin layer of game.**
- The loop's job is to create a new, specific want every minute or two.
- Skill is kept so low that owning more or rolling better is the only way to satisfy that want.
- The Robux option sits exactly where the want appears.
- Spending never hits a ceiling.

**Being simple to play is the mechanism, not a weakness they overcame.** When skill can't decide outcomes, purchases do. In a complex game, a losing player practices. Here there's nothing to practice, so the losing player buys.

Your metric, earnings relative to CCU, is effectively **Robux per hour played**. Every feature in these games pushes up one of four factors:

> **Robux per player-hour = wants created per hour × share of wants with a Robux option on screen × conversion per want × average price**

Hours played are the denominator, so anything that adds hours without creating wants (AFK farming, idle rewards) pulls the ratio down.

## Why simple games win on this metric

- **Purchases are the only lever.** With no skill to improve, the shop is the only way to progress.
- **New players reach a paid problem fast.** There's no tutorial or learning curve, so a new player hits something worth paying for within minutes, while still excited from the video that brought them. I'd bet most of Brainrot's and Pyramid's revenue comes from players' first one or two sessions.
- **Content is cheap.** A cutscene, a photo card, a portrait with reused effects, a recolored explosion or pyramid. Cheap content means huge collections, constant limited drops, and frequent reasons to come back.
- **The effort goes into monetization.** All five are built from the same standard modules: packs or eggs, potions, gift buttons, friend boosts, codes, login calendars, timed offers. The game itself is thin; the shop is thick.
- **Monetization buys discovery.** The Top Earning chart you're studying is also where players find games. A high ratio earns a spot on it, the spot brings traffic, and the effect compounds.

## If you copy only five things

1. A loop where skill doesn't decide outcomes, with a frustration the player hits in the first five minutes.
2. A Robux fix at the exact spot of every want.
3. A cheap purchase that can be bought again and again, always on screen.
4. A gacha with visible odds, bulk sizes, pity, and several named items in the top tier.
5. Permanent passes that make players use up consumables faster.

## Part 1: How the game itself drives spending

Ranked by how much I think each one contributes.

**1. Take skill out of the outcome.** All five decide results by stats, items or luck:
- Brainrot: walls you can't jump solo.
- Kpop Idol: pure-RNG spins.
- Anime Ball: auto-battles with no input after aiming.
- Blade Ball: one-button blocking (some skill remains, but abilities swing rounds).
- Pyramid: carrying blocks.

→ **Rule:** pick loops where practicing doesn't help. Friction should be **social** (needs other players), **statistical** (your numbers are too low) or **temporal** (waiting), never mechanical difficulty. Social friction is the best of the three: friends are the free fix, and every friend a player brings in is new traffic.

**2. Sell relief from a frustration the game creates, and access to something it puts on display.**

| Game | Frustration it creates | Desire it puts on display |
|---|---|---|
| Enter Brainrot | strangers abandon you on walls; the climb resets | 24 funny endings; giant players |
| Kpop Idol | slow spins, restock timers | your favorite idol; animated cards; neighbors' plots |
| Anime Ball | losing with a random weak hand | specific characters' effects on the lobby screens |
| Blade Ball | the ball outruns your reflexes | abilities and explosions seen from the spectator floor |
| Pyramid | a delivery bottleneck against a 357K-block pyramid | sky-high block towers; the 250x gym |

The frustration side sells skips, speed and power, which is how you get the *first* purchase. The desire side sells rolls and cosmetics, which is where the *deep* spending comes from.

→ **Rule:** write down your game's frustration and desire before building anything. Each needs a Robux fix and a free path that's far slower.

**3. Make the free path visibly 25–150× too long, and show the scale from the first minute.**
- About 20 runs for one Nuke.
- 100+ wins to max one ability.
- 100 pyramids for the 100x gym.
- A 357,760-block pyramid in front of a player who can carry 4.

The free path's job is to keep people around and hopeful, not to deliver the best items.

→ **Rule:** the free route to the best item takes 25–150 core cycles, and the first paid shortcut costs under 50 R.

**4. Create a new want every 1–3 minutes.** Each short cycle ends in a reveal that shows what you *didn't* get: a cutscene, a card stopping one slot past your idol, a 30-second battle, a 60–90-second round, a finished pyramid.

→ **Rule:** keep the core cycle between 30 seconds and 3 minutes, ending with a reveal and a visible next target.

**5. Use up or reset what players buy, so the want comes back.** Single-use weapons and a checkpoint reset; rebirths that wipe cash and packs; rounds that reset; pyramids that restart; boosts that expire after 15 minutes.

→ **Rule:** sell things that get used up, expire, or are needed again next cycle.

**6. Never let the numbers or the collection end.**
- Stacked multipliers: rarity × mutation × weight × grade × rebirth in Kpop Idol; gym × server boost × pool × pets in Pyramid.
- Collection indexes that grow with each update.
- All-time leaderboards.

→ **Rule:** at least three multiplier layers, or a collection that grows on a schedule. No max level.

**7. Keep everyone in front of everyone.** Battles on lobby screens, a spectator floor over the arena, income plots, giant avatars and block towers, purchase announcements. Enter Brainrot didn't even allow private lobbies. Free players are the audience that every status purchase needs.

→ **Rule:** public servers by default, spectating after elimination, and other players' best items placed where people wait.

**8. Build around a content unit that takes about an hour to make.** Release new ones on a visible countdown, from 30-minute banners to 2-week doors.

→ **Rule:** if a new collectible takes more than a day to make, the unit is too expensive for this model.

## Part 2: How the shop and buyable features drive spending

Ranked again.

**1. Put the Robux fix exactly where the want appears.**
- Brainrot's weapon pedestals offer the smallest cash pack that covers your shortfall.
- Kpop Idol and Pyramid put a Robux button beside every coin price.
- Anime Ball has upsell buttons inside the gacha window; Blade Ball has a Robux tab inside the ability shop.
- Pyramid's top bar sells blocks while building and extra minutes during the pool.
- Brainrot shows "Left Behind By Them? Get Revenge" the instant someone jumps off your head.
- Shop objects sit in the world, on paths players already walk: pedestals, crates, the wheel, gym pads, character statues.

→ **Rule:** a player should never have to leave the problem to find the fix. Work out exactly what they're missing, offer that, and make it one click.

**2. Always have a cheap repeatable purchase on screen.** Skip Stage for 19 R, Refill for 9 R, a 39 R roll, a 30 R discounted spin, a 29 R upgrade skip, a 49 R server boost. Every game had one, visible from the start. Game passes cap what a player can spend; repeatable products don't.

→ **Rule:** at least one 9–49 R repeatable product on the HUD at all times.

**3. Add a random-reward product with no spending ceiling, even if your game has nothing to do with randomness.** All five had one: Brainrot's mystery box, Kpop's packs, Anime Ball's gacha, Blade Ball's wheel and crates, Pyramid's eggs. Copy what the best of them do:
- **Visible odds**, with sizes of 1 / 10 / 100 and bigger bundles behind a "More" button. Brainrot's ×1,000 box is 16,000 R.
- **A bulk size at least as big as the short pity** (the number of rolls that guarantees a high-tier item), so no bulk purchase feels wasted. Anime Ball's 10-pull always includes a Godly.
- **A long pity at about 1 ÷ the odds** for the chase tier (the rarest items). It costs you almost nothing and works as a progress bar on spending.
- **A chase tier at 0.05–0.5%**, with an expected cost of 5,000–50,000 R to get a specific item. That's where the 51K and 61K top spenders live.
- **Several named items in the top tier.** If a tier holds five items and a player wants one specific item, they pay about 5× what it costs to reach the tier. This is what the fandom games exploit: players want Gojo, not just "a Void."
- **A visual upgrade for chase items** (animated, holographic, custom effects). It's the cheapest way to make the top tier look worth it.

**4. Make permanent passes speed up consumable spending.**
- Brainrot's Magic Carpet means more runs, and more runs use more single-use weapons.
- Quick Roll burns through packs much faster.
- Instant Spin burns through spins faster.
- A 250x gym makes every server boost and pool minute worth 125× more to its owner than to a 2x player.

→ **Rule:** before adding a pass, ask which consumable it makes players use up faster. If the answer is none, it's a one-time sale with a ceiling.

**5. Price as a ladder from impulse buy to whale, and make the top rung the best value.**

| Rung | Price | Examples seen |
|---|---|---|
| Impulse | 9–49 R | refills, stage skips, single rolls, upgrade skips |
| Small | 59–199 R | coin packs, partial-fix passes, pet slots |
| Core | 229–399 R | 10-pulls, main passes, 2x currency |
| Premium | 559–1,499 R | Magic Carpet, Sun God, top gyms, 100-packs |
| Whale | 900–16,000 R | ×50 / ×100 / ×1K bundles behind "More" |

The top rung should be the obvious smart buy. The carpet pays for itself by the third skip. The 250x gym costs about 6 R per point of multiplier, against about 20 R for the 5x.

→ **Also design for the leftover balance.** A kid with 0 R who buys a 19 R skip has to buy the smallest Robux pack (500 R in your screenshot) and is left with about 480 R. Have several items under that, and one attractive item at 399–449 R.

**6. Win the first purchase in the first session.**
- Blade Ball: a 60 R pack (coins, an ability and VIP, "worth 999") on a 35-minute countdown pinned to the HUD.
- Anime Ball: one-time bundles at 39 / 99 / 399 R and a doubled first purchase.
- Both: Day-2 login rewards that pull players into a second session.

→ **Rule:** one one-time offer at 60 R or less, obviously worth many times its price, on a 30–60-minute timer that starts at first join.

**7. Sell speed and time, and give free trials.** Mobility passes, Quick and Auto Roll, Instant Spin, Fast and Triple Hatch, Quick Pickup. Then hand out samples:
- Brainrot's skip token for inviting friends.
- Blade Ball's 30-minute Instant Spin from the wheel.
- Anime Ball's free rolls from playtime gifts.

→ **Rule:** every speed pass gets a free timed trial somewhere in the reward loop.

**8. Sell things other players see or feel.** Kill explosions that play on the victim's screen. Animated cards. Giant towers. Purchases announced in gold text. Server-wide boosts that help everyone but help the biggest investors most. Gift buttons on every item.

→ **Rule:** prefer purchases that make the server better (boosts, gifts, cosmetics) over ones that hurt other players.

**9. Put timers on content and on spending tracks.** Rotating banners (from 30 minutes to 3 days, with "won't return" warnings), 7-day limited capsules, and weekly spin milestones at 10 / 25 / 50. Fifty is exactly the average number of spins needed to hit Blade Ball's 2% Rapture.

→ **Rule:** one short rotation (an hour or less), one medium (3–7 days), and one weekly spending track.

**10. Ship the standard retention kit.** It's cheap and every game had it: a login calendar with big rewards on day 2 and the last day, playtime gifts, quests, codes (your hook for YouTubers), a group-join reward, and friend boosts with invite rewards.

## Blueprint for your next game

1. **Build the monetization kit once.** It should include:
   - A shop with tabs, including a Robux tab.
   - Checkout that offers exactly what the player is short.
   - A gacha module with odds, bulk sizes, pity and server announcements.
   - Timed one-time offers.
   - A login calendar, playtime gifts, quests and codes.
   - Friend boosts, gifting, leaderboards and analytics.

   After that, every new game is just a new loop, a new content unit and a theme. That's how "simple to make" becomes repeatable.
2. **Pick the loop.** Skill doesn't decide outcomes; cycles of 30 seconds to 3 minutes end in a reveal; servers are public; something gets used up or reset. Use one small map where every area is either part of the loop or a place to see other players and buy.
3. **Write down four things:** the frustration, the desire, the cheap fix that wins the first purchase, and the uncapped product that carries deep spending (usually the gacha).
4. **Set the numbers:**

| Parameter | Target |
|---|---|
| Core cycle | 30 s – 3 min, ending in a reveal |
| First friction skill can't fix | 2–5 min after joining, *after* the hook (Brainrot shows its cutscene first) |
| First Robux offer | at that friction, under 50 R |
| Free cycles to the best item | 25–150 |
| Cheapest repeatable product | 9–49 R, always on screen |
| Chase tier | 0.05–0.5%, pity ≈ 1 ÷ odds, 3–7 named items, expected cost 5K–50K R |
| Bulk sizes | 1 / 10 / 100, bulk ≥ short pity, ×50–×1K behind "More" |
| First-session offer | one-time, 60 R or less, 30–60 min timer |
| Rotations | banner of an hour or less, 3–7 day limited, weekly spending track |
| Login calendar | 5–7 days, big rewards on day 2 and the last day |

5. **Choose the theme for traffic.** The shop decides how much you make per player; the theme and creators decide whether you get players at all. These five are survivors: plenty of games with the same shop design never got traffic.
   - Ride a trend or a fandom.
   - Get the "favorite character" effect without IP from countries and flags, mythologies, animals, zodiac signs, or an invented roster with strong personalities.
   - Build 3–5 clip-worthy moments (an absurd cutscene, a 0.1% reveal, a giant number) plus a codes system, so your first ten minutes make a good YouTube video.
6. **Measure from day one.** Track a funnel (join → first want → first offer → first purchase), log purchases and currency flows, and track Robux per player-hour by product. Compare your own versions against each other rather than against outside benchmarks.

## What I'd skip or handle carefully

- **Griefing products** (Brainrot's Kill All, slap and sword). They sell, but they drive away the free players that every other mechanic depends on.
- **Top-spender boards that pin your own rank.** The effect is concentrated in a handful of whales, and with a young audience it's the feature most likely to draw complaints. Leaderboards for wins and collections give status without that risk.
- **Inflated "worth" prices and pressure timers aimed at kids.** Regulators take manipulative purchase flows seriously: the FTC's 2022 settlement with Epic included $245 million over tricking players into unwanted purchases. Keep your "worth" figures defensible.
- **Paid random items and trading.** Roblox requires showing odds before purchase. Also check Roblox's PolicyService for regions where paid random items or paid-item trading are restricted.
- **IP.** Real people's photos (Kpop Idol) and official anime art (Anime Ball) can get a game taken down overnight. The no-IP themes above get the same "favorite character" effect.
